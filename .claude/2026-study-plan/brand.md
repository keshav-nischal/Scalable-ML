# Reputation & LinkedIn Playbook

**Thesis:** the goal is *not* to look employable — it's to be genuinely interesting to other people who love this work. Do that consistently and the right inbound (experts who want to talk to you, recruiters who trust what they see) follows on its own. The moment content reads as *"posted to impress recruiters,"* sharp readers discount it — and recruiters are sharp readers. So the strategy **is** authenticity: post from real curiosity and honest observations, not from an achievement checklist. ~50 credible, human posts over 6 months, none of them performative.

**The one rule everything else serves:** *post the thing you'd actually say to a friend who's also into this.* Not "look what I accomplished" — "huh, look at this weird thing I found." The best technical readers are humans first; a small, true, well-observed detail beats a grand announcement every time.

**Your two real differentiators — let them surface naturally, don't lead every post with them:**
1. **GSoC 2024 contributor** — public merged PRs speak for themselves. On the profile, label it *"Google Summer of Code 2024 Contributor, [Org]"* (org as affiliation, never "Google" as employer). In posts, mention it only when it's genuinely relevant.
2. **ex-SDET** — you instinctively ask *"how do we know this is actually right?"* That question is your natural obsession, and it happens to be the exact thing LLM teams fear getting wrong. Let it show through what you're curious about (eval, failure cases, honest metrics), not through a job-title flex.

## Profile = landing page, not résumé

The profile can be deliberate — it *is* where someone lands to evaluate you. Keep it clear, but keep it sounding like a person.

- **Headline:** `Building reliable LLM/ML systems | RAG · LLM evaluation · MLOps | ex-SDET · GSoC 2024 | Open to freelance`
- **Featured (3 pinned proofs):** best repo (P3 RAG API), a reproduction writeup (nanoGPT), a quantified project (P4 eval pipeline). Update as you ship.
- **About:** write it like you talk. Open with something you actually believe ("Most LLM demos work in the notebook and quietly fall apart in production — I'm more interested in the falling-apart part."), say what you do, end with a low-key invitation to talk.
- **Experience:** SDET framed honestly as reliability/quality work that transfers to ML. GSoC with the org + a link to merged PRs.

## Content engine (from the work, never invented)

Forget the pillars-and-angles matrix — that's how you get mechanical posts. Instead, **keep a running notes file of moments.** Every study session leaves at least one:

- **A surprise** — "I expected X, got Y." (These are gold. Genuine surprise is the most engaging thing you can post.)
- **A gotcha** — the bug/pitfall that cost you two hours and will cost someone else two hours.
- **A small delight** — the thing that made you grin (a net that finally learns, a clean diagram, an elegant idea underneath a scary-sounding paper).
- **An honest confusion** — "I still don't fully get why this works." Asking a real question pulls experts in to answer; it doesn't make you look weak, it makes you look like someone actually doing the work.

Most posts are just one of these, written plainly, with the artifact attached (a plot, a screenshot, five lines of code, a before/after number). You never run out, because you're not inventing topics — you're reporting what happened.

> The test for any post: *would I say this to a friend over coffee?* If it sounds like a press release, kill it. If it sounds like "okay this is kind of cool / kind of annoying / kind of surprising," post it.

**Two streams that are pure gold for you — these will produce most of your non-milestone posts:**

1. **Weekend builds (Sat/Sun).** No job those days, so use a slice of it to pick up a tool you're curious about, build or break something small with it, and post the surprise. *"Spent Saturday poking at [tool] — here's the one thing I didn't expect."* Low-stakes, high-personality, shows range without trying to. This is the most Karpathy-shaped thing you can do.
2. **Field notes from pointing AI agents at never-tested tools (from your day job).** You're *already* doing this and *already* finding things — that is the content, and almost nobody posts from this angle. *"I've been having agents manually test tools nobody's stress-tested before. Here's a failure pattern that keeps showing up."* It's hands-on, genuinely novel, and it lands squarely on your eval/reliability instinct without you ever having to name-drop "ex-SDET." **One caveat:** post the *generalizable pattern*, never employer- or client-confidential specifics — anonymize the tool, keep the insight. When in doubt, leave it out.

## Voice — steal from Karpathy (your north star)

You already love how he writes; trust that instinct — it's the whole style guide. Distilled:

- **Lead with genuine "huh, this is cool."** Real enthusiasm, never manufactured hype. If you're not actually interested, don't post it.
- **Be concrete.** Show the actual thing — five lines of code, the number, the screenshot, the failing output. The specifics *are* the point.
- **Think out loud.** Post the half-formed idea and the "I don't fully get why this works yet." Narrating your own learning is the content — it invites experts to fill the gap, rather than daring them to correct a claim.
- **Explain hard things plainly.** If you can't say it simply you don't understand it yet — and it's fine to say exactly that.
- **Give an idea a clean handle** when one's there (a crisp analogy, a memorable phrase, the way he coined "software 2.0" or "vibe coding") — but never force a catchphrase.
- **One idea, short.** Cut the throat-clearing, the "excited to share," and the CTAs.
- **Low ego, high curiosity.** Own the mistakes out loud; credit others generously.
- **No bait.** Minimal hashtags/emojis, zero "Agree? 👇". The substance is the hook. Casual, even lowercase, is fine — it sounds like a person, not a brand.

## Formats & algorithm (2026)

- **PDF carousels / document posts** = highest engagement (saves + reposts drive reach). Good for the "here's a thing I built and what I learned" milestone posts.
- **First-person, specific, honest** beats generic AI explainers every time. "Here's what surprised me" > "Here are 5 tips about RAG."
- Strong, plain **first-150-char hook** — say the surprising thing first, don't tease it. Put external **links in the first comment**, not the body.
- **Skip** polls and "Agree? 👇" bait (spam-classified, and it's exactly the try-hard energy you're avoiding). Video is secondary; carousels primary.
- **Comments are half your reach and most of your credibility.** A genuinely useful reply on someone else's post — a real observation, not "great post!" — does more for you than a mediocre original post.

## Cadence (fits 12–15 hrs/wk)

- **2–3 posts/week + daily genuine comments.** Don't force the third post — a real one beats a filler one.
- **Weekends are your exploration window** (no job Sat/Sun): spend a slice on a tool you're curious about or on writing up an agent-testing find, then post the surprise. This is where most of your non-milestone posts will come from — the study-week milestones are the backbone, the weekend builds are the personality.
- **One ~60-min weekly block** (part of your Sunday ritual): turn the week's captured moments into posts. If nothing that week is worth posting, post less — silence beats noise.
- **80/20:** 80% engaging in your niche, 20% original posting.
- **Post from the work, not on top of it:** the moments file makes this automatic. If you have to sit and *think up* a post, that's the signal you've drifted into performative mode.

## Beyond LinkedIn (compounding assets)

- **GitHub profile README** = your real portfolio. Pin the anchor 3. Green CI badges. Clear per-project READMEs.
- **A one-page portfolio site** (GitHub Pages) — demos, repros, posts, contact. Built in W23.
- **Cross-post reproduction writeups** to a blog / dev.to; consider the **NeurIPS 2026 ML Reproducibility Challenge** for a citable credential.
- **Open source:** a few well-scoped PRs to a popular LLM/eval library (LangChain, RAGAS, DeepEval, llama-index) — extends your GSoC signal and puts your name in maintainers' view. Do these because a bug genuinely annoys you, not for the résumé line — it reads better and it's more fun.

## Converting to freelance (late Dec → 2027)

- **Braintrust** (usebraintrust.com) — most freelancer-favorable: flat **15% client-side fee, you keep 100%.** Best home base.
- **Upwork** — largest, but the generic "AI/ML" pool is over-fished. **Win by niching** (e.g. "RAG + LLM evaluation for [vertical]") — specific beats generic.
- **Gun.io / Toptal** — curated, higher rates, heavier vetting/markup. Worth applying once you have the anchor 3.
- **Positioning > rate-cutting.** Sell outcomes and reliability, not hours. Package the work (fixed-scope RAG build, an "LLM eval audit") rather than billing hourly.
- Your **"LLM eval audit"** could be a signature productized offer — cheap to deliver, high-trust, leads to bigger builds. Few competitors offer it, and it's the natural extension of the question you actually care about.
